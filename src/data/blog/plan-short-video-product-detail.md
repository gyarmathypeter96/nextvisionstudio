A short product video works best when it helps a particular customer understand one detail that matters to a buying decision. That detail could be how a product is used, how it looks in context, what a material feels like or what makes one version different. Choose the point before production, then decide whether movement, a person’s explanation or atmosphere makes it clearer than a photograph alone.

The Macaron Boutique project began while I was in Alicante. The products and atmosphere stood out, so I approached the business and developed the creative direction around macarons, coffee, colour and the boutique’s French character. The finished social video used close details and a calm rhythm. It was a self-started international project, not an example of a typical Irish production budget. ## Choose the detail that helps someone decide

Ask what the viewer needs to notice. For food, it may be texture, colour or the moment a product is served. For a practical item, it may be a feature that solves a small everyday problem. For a service product, the useful point might be how a customer starts or what happens after purchase.

Write that one point in plain language. Then decide whether the video should demonstrate it, show it in context or let someone explain it. Video is useful when movement, sequence or atmosphere adds information that a photograph cannot show.

## Prepare the product and the space

The product needs to look and work as intended. Check labels, packaging, cleanliness and any parts that should appear on screen. If the item is food, confirm which version will be presented and how it should be handled between takes. For a demonstration, rehearse the action once so the team knows how long it takes and which part viewers should see.

The background, surfaces and nearby objects all become part of the story. A boutique can benefit from its own shelves and counter; an ecommerce product may need a quieter surface that keeps attention on the item. The creative should fit the brand and the place rather than borrowing an unrelated visual style.

## Match the format to the channel

For social media, a vertical edit can make the product easy to view on a phone. A website product page may need a horizontal version or a longer demonstration. Paid advertising may require a concise opening, a clear offer and a next step. These formats can sometimes come from the same production, but they need to be agreed before editing begins.

## Make the opening easy to understand

The first moments should make the subject clear. Show the product, the problem it solves or the detail the video will reveal. A visual opening can be enough when the image is immediately recognisable; otherwise, a few words on screen can help.

The middle should support that promise. If the opening suggests a close look at the product, follow through with the details. If the video starts with a customer problem, show how the product fits into the answer. The ending can give a practical next step: browse a collection, ask a question or visit a shop.

## How do you decide what a product video should show?

Start with the customer’s decision. What would they want to see before choosing, ordering or visiting? For food, it may be texture, colour, serving or scale. For a practical item, it may be a feature in use. For a handmade product, the material and making process may matter. A service package may need to show the steps a customer can expect rather than a physical object.

Write the point in a single sentence, such as “show how the lid closes securely” or “help a customer understand the size beside an everyday object”. Then choose the visual that proves or explains that point. A close detail can show finish; a wider composition can explain scale; a hand or person can establish use. Do not add props simply because a set looks empty. Every visible object should support the intended impression.

## What should be prepared before filming?

Make sure the product is clean, complete and working as intended. Check labels, packaging, variants and any small details that must appear accurately. Bring more than one sample if a product may be handled, served or arranged repeatedly. If the finish changes with light or temperature, agree how to maintain a consistent appearance during the session.

Plan the setting around where customers encounter the product. A boutique’s shelves and counter may help tell a story about the place. An online listing may need a quieter background that makes the product easy to compare. A use demonstration needs enough space to show the action clearly. The styling should make the product legible and truthful; a staged context should not imply features or included accessories that the customer will not receive.

For food or cosmetics, confirm handling and presentation requirements with the business. For products with safety instructions, make sure the demonstration is correct. If a person’s hands or face appear, agree consent and any required releases. These checks are small compared with the time lost if an important detail is missing or shown inaccurately.

## How should you plan the sequence of a short product video?

A useful sequence can be simple: introduce the product, show the chosen detail, place it in use or context, then give the viewer a next step. The opening should make the subject clear right away. The middle should deliver what the opening promised. The final frame can point to a product page, collection, shop or enquiry route.

For a vertical social edit, consider how the product will sit on a phone screen and leave room for platform controls or captions. A website product page may need a horizontal version or a longer demonstration. Paid advertising may need a specific opening, offer and destination. ## How can product content answer common objections?

Ask sales staff what customers want to check before buying. Is the size hard to judge? Is an ingredient or material unfamiliar? Does the customer want to see how the item opens, fits, pours or works? Each repeated question can become a useful piece of content. Answer it accurately, and send the viewer to a page with full specifications, care guidance and availability.

Do not hide important limitations in a fast edit. If the product comes in several sizes, make clear which one is shown. If a demonstration requires an accessory, identify whether it is included. If colours vary on different screens, state that on the product page. A video can reduce uncertainty when it shows real details; it cannot replace information the buyer needs to compare options.

### Frequently asked questions

#### Is video better than product photography?

It depends on what a customer needs to understand. A still photo can show appearance clearly; video helps when movement, use, sequence, sound or atmosphere adds useful information. Many product pages use both for different purposes.

#### How many products should be included in one session?

There is no universal number. It depends on styling changes, demonstrations, locations and the number of finished edits. Group similar products together and agree the deliverables in advance.

#### Can one product video work on every platform?

Possibly, but dimensions, opening, captions and calls to action may need changes for different destinations. Name the channels in the brief and confirm which versions are included.
