Good product photography removes doubt. It shows what the customer is buying, what makes it different and how it fits into their life.

The fastest way to waste a product shoot is to arrive with a box of products and no agreed image list. You may leave with attractive photographs, but still miss the exact angles, details or formats needed for the website and campaign.

Use this shot list to plan a professional product photography session for a Dublin business.

## Begin with the product page

Start with the place closest to the sale. For most products, that is the website product page.

A customer should be able to understand the product without filling gaps in their imagination. The essential image set usually includes:

1. A clear front view.
2. A rear view when it contains useful detail.
3. Both side angles where shape or controls matter.
4. A three-quarter hero angle.
5. A close-up of the most important feature.
6. Packaging and everything included.
7. An image that shows scale.

Not every product needs every angle. A candle and a piece of technical equipment answer different customer questions. The rule is simple: photograph the details someone would inspect if the product were in their hands.

## Capture one clean hero image

The hero image is the clearest, most recognisable view of the product. It often appears first on a product page, collection page, advertisement or sales presentation.

Keep the background, lighting and angle consistent across a range. Customers can compare products more easily, and the website feels more professional.

Plan at least two hero compositions:

- a tight version where the product fills the frame;
- a wider version with clean space for a headline, price or button.

That second image is especially useful for website banners and paid campaigns. Trying to add text to a tightly cropped product photograph usually creates a weak compromise.

## Show the details that justify the choice

Close-up images are useful when quality lives in the detail.

Consider photographing:

- texture and finish;
- stitching, engraving or materials;
- controls, connections or moving parts;
- ingredients or labels;
- closures and packaging;
- handmade features;
- the product in use at close range.

Do not photograph details just to increase the image count. Each close-up should answer a question, prove quality or support a claim made in the product copy.

If a feature is difficult to explain in one still image, plan a short demonstration video during the same production. A connected [video production shoot](/videography-dublin/) can show movement, setup and use while the products and lighting are already prepared.

## Include scale and context

Customers often abandon a product page because they cannot judge size, proportion or real-world use.

Scale can be shown with:

- a hand holding the product;
- the product beside a familiar object;
- a person wearing or using it;
- the product placed in its intended environment;
- a simple dimension graphic prepared after the shoot.

Context images should still keep the product easy to identify. A beautiful lifestyle scene is not useful when the viewer cannot tell what is being sold.

## Build a lifestyle and advertising set

Once the essential sales images are covered, create photographs that give the product a mood and a reason to care.

A useful campaign set can include:

- one strong brand hero image;
- the product in a realistic setting;
- a person using it naturally;
- a grouped range or collection image;
- an overhead arrangement;
- a vertical social image;
- a square advertisement crop;
- a wide image with negative space for copy;
- seasonal versions only when they fit the campaign calendar.

The setting should support the positioning. Premium products need controlled details. Food and drink need freshness and appetite. Practical products need clarity and believable use.

## Plan images for every important format

One composition rarely works perfectly everywhere.

Add these formats to the shot list before the photographer sets the frame:

- horizontal 16:9 for website banners and presentations;
- square 1:1 for product grids and social posts;
- vertical 4:5 for Instagram and Meta campaigns;
- vertical 9:16 for Stories, Reels covers and mobile ads;
- clean product crops for marketplaces or catalogues.

Cropping after the shoot is useful, but it cannot recover missing background or move a product that was placed too close to the edge.

## Prepare the products before photography

Retouching should polish a strong product, not rebuild a poorly prepared one.

Before the shoot:

- choose the cleanest final examples;
- bring backups for fragile or perishable products;
- remove price stickers and temporary labels;
- clean dust, fingerprints and packaging marks;
- check that labels are straight and consistent;
- prepare every colour and size that genuinely needs its own image;
- list any details that must not be altered in editing;
- confirm how products will arrive and leave the studio or location.

Small marks become obvious under controlled lighting. Preparation is usually faster and more accurate than heavy retouching later.

## Group the shot list by setup

Do not organise the day only by product name. Group it by lighting, background and camera angle.

For example:

1. Photograph all clean front views on the same background.
2. Complete the matching side and detail images.
3. Change to the lifestyle setup.
4. Create grouped range images.
5. Finish with any people, hands or action shots.

This structure reduces repeated setup changes and helps maintain consistency across the product range.

Mark each shot as essential, useful or optional. If time changes, the photographer knows what must be completed first.

## Agree the editing and delivery standard

Before the session, confirm:

- the number of selected final photographs;
- colour correction and retouching expectations;
- whether backgrounds need to be pure white, natural or transparent;
- file dimensions and formats;
- web and print versions;
- naming conventions for products and variants;
- the review and approval process;
- commercial usage requirements.

Consistent file names save real time when images are uploaded to a shop or shared with a marketing team. Use product names, SKUs or another system the business already understands.

## A practical product photography checklist

Send this information with the enquiry:

- number of products and variants;
- link to the current website or product page;
- essential angles for each product;
- details customers need to inspect;
- examples of the visual style;
- required backgrounds and props;
- website, advertising and social formats;
- people or locations involved;
- delivery deadline;
- usage and campaign plans.

You do not need to make every creative decision alone. A good photographer will use the list to recommend the simplest setup that covers the real business need.
