At Malahide Marina on 13 November 2026, it was cold enough that I felt it every time I stopped moving. The day kept going: people arriving, putting on lifejackets, talking, getting involved and giving me a few moments for an interview before returning to the activity. My job for Collen Construction was to bring that experience back in films and photographs that felt like the people who were there.

There are three finished films from the project. **Collen 01 — Volunteers’ Experience** brings the day together, and Collen has already used it on LinkedIn. The other two are separate interviews with David and Rebecca. You can watch all three on this page. They show how one day of [event videography](/event-videographer-dublin/) can produce an overview of an event alongside more personal accounts.

## What did Collen need the videos to show?

The people were the centre of this project. A harbour gives you an attractive setting, but a view of the water cannot explain why someone has turned up or what it is like to take part. The film needed both: enough of the location to place the viewer there, and enough time with participants to give the activity a human voice.

Collen is an established construction business. Its [official website](https://www.collen.com/) describes work across Ireland, the UK and Europe. It was a privilege to make content for the team. For this particular brief, the story was a volunteering day away from the usual construction setting. Showing people doing something together gave the film a clear focus.

This is a useful approach to [corporate video production](/corporate-video-production-dublin/) when a business wants to communicate its culture. The strongest material often comes from a specific occasion: the work people do, the things they notice and the way they speak about an experience. A real day gives the viewer something concrete to understand.

## What happened at Malahide Marina?

The activity involved volunteers counting oysters into bundles, filling baskets and helping with their deployment at the marina. That sequence gave the filming a practical structure. I could follow the work from preparation through to the water, while keeping the people doing it in view. Small actions mattered here: hands working through the material, people leaning in to help and the concentration around a basket.

The brief called for Collen volunteers taking part, a group portrait and photographs of the work in progress. The finished group image brings everyone together beside the boats; the closer photographs return to individual moments. Those two scales help a viewer understand both the group effort and what participation actually involved.

For a business planning an outdoor event in [Malahide or North Dublin](/videographer-malahide/), this is a useful starting point: identify the activity that makes the day worth documenting. A location gives the film its setting, but the practical sequence gives it something to follow.

## Filming at a harbour in very cold conditions

The cold was a practical part of the production. I had to stay ready while moving with the event. My camera kept working through the conditions, so I could keep following what mattered on the day.

Working outdoors also changes what you pay attention to. People turn towards a conversation, an activity begins beside the water, or a group gathers briefly before moving on. Those moments do not wait for a perfect camera position. I needed to keep noticing what was happening around me and make decisions quickly.

The photographs show the contrast I wanted to preserve: bright water and boats, red lifejackets, people wrapped up against the cold, and hands busy with the activity. Those details give an outdoor event its own character. They also stop a business film from feeling interchangeable with another company’s day out.

## Keeping spontaneous interviews natural

Some of the interviews happened as opportunities appeared during the event. That brings a different energy from a fully scheduled conversation in an office. A participant is speaking from inside the experience, with the activity still close at hand. The aim is to give them room to say something useful without making a brief conversation feel like a performance.

David and Rebecca have their own films. Keeping those interviews separate allows a viewer to spend more time with each person, while the volunteers’ film can move between people and activity. It is a practical way to give the same event different depths: a short introduction to the day and individual voices for someone who wants to hear more.

For a similar [interview-led business video](/corporate-video-production-dublin/), it helps to agree the important themes before the event. Then the conversation can stay natural. Trying to force everyone into identical answers usually removes the very differences that make their contributions worth hearing.

## Making room for moments you cannot plan

A busy event involves more people and conversations than a single camera can follow at once. The task is to recognise which moments help a viewer understand the day. That can be a group gathered together, someone concentrating on the work, or the short exchange that happens before an interview begins.

I had to balance the important activity with what was unfolding around it. A plan is useful for knowing what must be covered, but the finished story also depends on responding to the room — or, in this case, the harbour. That is one of the parts of live filming I enjoy: being prepared enough to notice the unexpected.

The [Gut Fest event film](/case-studies/gut-fest-event-videography-dublin/) involved a different setting and programme, but the same need to connect separate moments into a day that makes sense. For Collen, the water, the volunteers and their own words gave me that connection.

## Where the drone coverage fits

I also filmed with a drone. Aerial coverage adds a sense of the wider location that is difficult to get while standing beside the activity. At a harbour, that perspective can help explain the relationship between the water, the surrounding space and the people below.

The closer footage still carries the personal story. A wide view tells the audience where they are; a conversation or a detail of someone working tells them what to pay attention to. The value is in moving between those perspectives with a purpose. The drone material was one part of the coverage, alongside the filming and photography on the ground.

If you are considering aerial coverage for an event, include it in the initial [production brief](/event-videographer-dublin/). The location and conditions affect what can be planned, so it should be discussed as part of the actual day rather than treated as an automatic extra.

## Why make three films from one event?

An overview and an interview answer different questions. The overview helps a viewer quickly understand the occasion. An interview lets one person explain their experience in more detail. Separating them makes each film easier to use without asking one edit to do everything.

The delivered files include **16:9 landscape and 1:1 square versions** of each film. The landscape versions are available on this page. Providing the agreed formats gives the client options for placing the content, while retaining the same underlying story. It does not mean every version needs to be posted everywhere at once.

For businesses planning [short-form video content](/short-form-video-production-dublin/), the important question is what each edit will help someone understand. Here, the three pieces have distinct subjects: the volunteers’ shared experience, David’s interview and Rebecca’s interview. That is a clearer reason for multiple deliverables than simply asking for more clips.

## Photographs that carry the same story

The photography keeps the same attention on people and participation. The group portrait records who came together. Closer images show what being there looked like: warm layers, lifejackets, conversation and work beside the water. Together, they give the event a visual record beyond the video edits.

This is why [event photography](/photography-dublin/) and filming can complement each other. A photograph lets a viewer stop with one moment, while a film can connect that moment to a voice or an action. Planning both around the same subject helps the finished material feel consistent.

The [Dublin SaaS networking photography project](/case-studies/saas-networking-photography-dublin/) has a similar emphasis on people and connection, in a very different environment. The location changes how I work, but the first question stays practical: what should someone who was not there be able to understand or feel?

## What this project delivered

The result is a short volunteers’ film, two individual interview films and photographs from a real day at the harbour. The client has already used Collen 01. David and Rebecca’s interviews add two individual perspectives alongside the main film.

For me, the important part was staying attentive through the cold and giving the people enough space in the finished work. The camera held up, the event kept moving, and the story came from what participants were actually doing and saying. That is the part I wanted a viewer to take away.
