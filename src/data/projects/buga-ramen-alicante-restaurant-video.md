Walking into Buga Ramen in Alicante changes the setting around you. Outside is a Spanish city; inside are Japanese lanterns, anime references, bright signs and bowls of ramen. My brief was to carry that feeling into a short restaurant video. The food needed to look inviting, and the room needed to explain why eating here feels different from ordering an ordinary takeaway.

That sense of stepping into another place guided the work. I wanted the viewer to notice the restaurant’s own character before seeing a message telling them to visit. The finished film moves between the menu, food and interior details, giving the audience a close look at the experience. This is an Alicante project in my international portfolio; the same approach to a business’s individual character informs my [short-form video production in Dublin](/short-form-video-production-dublin/).

## A local restaurant within an international brand

Buga Ramen is an established Japanese restaurant chain with an international presence in Spain and Portugal. Its [official Spanish website](https://buga-ramen.com/) lists restaurants in cities including Alicante, Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia, Bilbao and Seville. The [Portuguese website](https://bugaramenportugal.pt/) presents the same combination of Japanese food and an immersive setting. That gives the Alicante restaurant a wider brand context while leaving the individual space as the subject of this film.

It was a privilege to create work for the Alicante restaurant within that wider brand. The responsibility was to represent what makes the experience recognisable: the food, the visual references and the atmosphere inside. Calling the business international should help explain the commission. The film still needed to show a real restaurant that a person in Alicante could choose to visit.

## The creative question: what happens when you cross the doorway?

A restaurant can describe itself with a cuisine, menu and address. Video has another useful job: showing how it feels to be there. For Buga Ramen, the doorway represents a change of atmosphere. The brief was about taking the viewer away from the street and into the restaurant’s Japanese-inspired world. That gave the film a more specific purpose than recording a collection of attractive dishes.

The challenge was keeping the experience coherent. Lanterns, signs, characters and food all compete for attention in a lively interior. Selecting a few recognisable details gives the viewer a way through that visual information. The finished piece uses close views to make individual elements readable, then connects them through the rhythm of the edit. Each detail belongs to the same place rather than feeling like an unrelated image collected for decoration.

## Showing the food alongside the setting

The food was central to the brief. A viewer should understand that this is somewhere to eat, even when the interior is visually striking. Close views of dishes and accompaniments bring the attention back to the table. They introduce texture, colour and the practical reason for visiting: choosing something from the menu and enjoying it in that setting.

The bowl of ramen and the closer food details give the film a practical centre. They bring the viewer back from the illustrated walls to the meal itself. That return matters: the setting invites curiosity, and the food gives that curiosity somewhere to go.

For food content, the framing needs to make the subject easy to recognise. A very tight detail can be beautiful while telling a new customer little about the dish. Context helps. The menu, serving pieces and surrounding table details give the food a place in the restaurant experience. A similar balance appears in my [Macaron Boutique product video](/case-studies/macaron-boutique-product-video/), where the products and the boutique’s character belong in the same story.

## Letting the interior communicate the culture

The lanterns and illustrated walls are prominent in the finished video. They matter because they are part of what a guest sees on arrival. The Japanese-inspired setting is already present in the restaurant; the production’s job was to notice it, frame it clearly and give the viewer enough time to take it in. Borrowing an unrelated visual style would have weakened that connection.

The cultural references also needed context. The film presents Buga Ramen’s own interpretation of Japanese food and pop culture in Alicante. It does not claim to document everyday life in Japan. That distinction allows the video to celebrate the restaurant honestly. The material is specific to this business, its design and its menu. For a wider hospitality brief, my [commercial photography service](/photography-dublin/) can provide complementary images that show a venue and its details in a different format.

## Working with colour, detail and pace

The restaurant’s colour is a major part of its identity. Bright signs sit beside warm lantern light and illustrated surfaces. If every cut tried to be the loudest moment, the viewer would have little opportunity to understand the room. A useful rhythm lets a detail register before the next one arrives. The edit can then build a sense of variety without making the space feel confusing.

The finished film offers several scales of detail: a menu, a wall illustration, hanging lanterns and food on the table. Those changes keep the eye moving while maintaining one subject. The creative decision is always about what the next image adds. This relationship between message, detail and duration is explored further in the [short-form video guide](/blog/short-form-video-production-dublin-guide/).

## Why a vertical restaurant film fits this brief

The archive contains a finished vertical film, which makes the close details easy to see on a phone. A vertical composition can give a dish, lantern or sign a strong place in the frame. It also encourages a focused choice: which element should a viewer notice first, and which parts of the room can remain in the background?

A vertical film still needs a beginning, development and ending. The format does not remove the need for a story. For Buga Ramen, the connection between food and atmosphere supplies that structure. A longer website film would have room for a different kind of explanation, such as a fuller tour or a conversation with the team. The [short-form versus long-form comparison](/blog/short-form-vs-long-form-video-dublin/) explains how I choose between those uses when planning a brief.

## The restaurant’s character shaped the production

The Buga Ramen film and the nearby [Pekado Kebab project](/case-studies/pekado-kebab-alicante-video/) both concern food businesses in Alicante, but their priorities differ. Buga Ramen’s story is about an immersive interior and the food within it. Pekado’s brief gives more attention to a new business’s exterior and distinctive food. Applying the same sequence to both would hide the reason each place is worth showing.

That is why the initial conversation matters. A producer needs to understand what guests remember, what the business wants to introduce and what should appear in the finished work. A useful [business video planning brief](/blog/business-video-production-planning-dublin/) turns those answers into production choices. It also prevents the filming day from becoming an attempt to collect every possible image with no shared priority.

## Planning a similar restaurant project

For a restaurant, I begin by asking what a first-time guest needs to understand. It might be the setting, the food, the team, a new opening or a seasonal menu. Agreeing that priority makes the rest of the brief easier: which dishes should be ready, when the room will look right, who can appear and which versions the business needs afterwards.

If photographs are required as well, I list them separately so there is time to create both formats properly.
